The Kansas City commercial real estate market enjoyed strong performance over the first three months of the year, with positive absorption for both office and industrial helping push vacancy rates lower. With office coming off its strongest year since the onset of the pandemic and a sizeable move-in set for later this year at Aspiria, it appears the office vacancy rate will continue to drop. On the industrial side, new development is starting to move forward so much of the attention will be focused on how quickly space in speculative projects is leased (or preleased) over the coming months.

Economic data in the first quarter was noisy, especially with the uncertainty caused by rising oil prices and global trade disruption having a major influence in March. The long-term impact of these factors remains to be seen, but in the short term the general trends have continued. Year-over-year (YOY), unemployment was up on a national and local level, although both numbers remain low by historical standards. Total non-farm employment was up nominally on a national basis—an increase of 20 basis points (bps)—YOY and down very slightly in the Kansas City area (10 bps), although forecasts call for job growth over the next 12 months.

#### MARKET OVERVIEW:

This should be a telling year for the Kansas City industrial market. After a strong expansion of about a decade, the national industrial market cooled off in 2024 and 2025. Kansas City followed a similar trend, with leasing activity peaking at 14.9 million square feet (msf) in 2021 and coming in at 12.4 msf in both 2022 and 2023 before sliding down to 11.1 msf and 10.9 msf in 2024 and 2025, respectively. However, absorption statistics told a slightly different story.

Last year’s absorption was powered by the completion of a once-in-lifetime manufacturing facility that had been under construction for multiple years and the delivery of large-scale built-to-suit (BTS) projects where the leg work and leases were done in 2023.

Most observers believe the industrial market is entering another period of expansion—although as mentioned above, Kansas City has not really gotten going yet despite some eye-popping stats from 2025. The big question is when will that momentum get started. Absorption for the first quarter was 1.8 msf, although over half of that came from the delivery of two BTS projects and a speculative building that was 100% pre-leased to two separate tenants. Leasing activity came in at 2.8 msf, which would have been the second highest total of any quarter in 2025. In the short term, leasing activity will be a better indicator of the direction of the market, with hopes that 2026 will see a number at or above 12.0 msf.

If leasing activity continues to creep towards the 3.0-msf (or more) perquarter level, development will accelerate. Aggressive speculative construction was the original driver of the last expansion, and while total under construction figures remained high in 2024 and early 2025, the composition changed. At the end of the first quarter of 2021, Kansas City had a total of 10.1 msf of space under construction, of which 75.3% was speculative (7.6 msf). At the midway point of 2024, Kansas City had 11.0 msf under construction, but only 14.2% of that figure was spec (1.6 msf). From the start of 2024 through the third quarter of 2025, Kansas City saw seven straight quarters where the amount of speculative space under construction was below 2.0 msf before finishing 2025 with 2.1 msf. New projects breaking ground in the first three months of the year helped push that total to 2.6 msf.

One trend that may point towards demand for new development is the increased vacancy in older, Class B Warehouse buildings. The Class B Warehouse vacancy rate ended 2019 at 7.7% but then dropped down to just 2.4% by the end of 2022. In contrast, the vacancy rate in Modern Distribution space was 5.9% at the end of 2019 and went down a bit more in 2020 before rising in each of the next three years to reach 9.4% and then spiking to 12.8% in 2024 as leasing activity in speculative buildings under construction slowed. However, that rate fell throughout 2025 and ended the first quarter of 2026 at just 7.3% while the Class B vacancy rose to just over 10.0%, its highest level in more than a decade. In the context of the larger market, this is significant. Overall vacancy for the market at the end of the first quarter was 5.9%, down 124 bps from year-end 2024. With Modern Distribution and overall vacancy falling and Class B Warehouse rising, it may be a sign that a portion of the 41.8-msf Class B Warehouse inventory will struggle to backfill space and newer product will be required to meet market demand.

Numerous industrial sites that have had proposed projects on hold are likely to see construction start over the next few quarters.

Demand for owner-user buildings up to 150,000 sf remains strong, while data center speculators are making land acquisitions murky.

Kansas City will begin welcoming visitors for the World Cup in early June, with the tournament running into the third quarter and some estimates putting the total number of visitors at around 650,000.
